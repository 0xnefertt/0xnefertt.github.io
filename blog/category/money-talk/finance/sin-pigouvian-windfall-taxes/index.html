Sin Tax, Pigouvian Tax, and Windfall Tax

0xnefertt2026.10.05.

What is the real reason we pay taxes?

Governments collect taxes to cover the shared costs of keeping a country functioning safely and enabling all its people to live well together. The basic purpose of tax revenue is the same across countries, but the types and characteristics of taxes differ. Today, I want to look at some rather unusual taxes.

1. Sin tax: A particularly high tax imposed on consumption considered socially harmful or undesirable. Typical examples include tobacco, alcohol, gambling, cannabis, and sugary drinks. The purpose of a sin tax is to discourage certain consumption, with a focus on reducing services that negatively affect society or consumption deemed undesirable. Apparently, there have also been cases of taxes on prostitution and the sex trade in the past.

Looking at the composition of sin taxes collected in Korea, tobacco taxes account for 70%, alcohol/liquor taxes for over 20%, and the remainder comes from gambling industries such as casinos, horse racing, and lotteries. On a KRW 4,500 pack of cigarettes, taxes and levies amount to 74% (KRW 3,233): tobacco consumption tax, the National Health Promotion levy, individual consumption tax, local education tax, value-added tax, and other charges. Someone smoking a pack a day is a taxpayer contributing over KRW 1 million a year through cigarettes alone.

As a nonsmoker, I can agree with raising cigarette prices and collecting taxes. But if prices rise to an absurd level and smokers quit, reducing revenue, the government will collect that money elsewhere. So I cannot say it is entirely a good thing. On the other hand, cigarettes dirty the streets and damage health, so I cannot simply encourage smoking either. Perhaps the e-cigarette industry could offer a somewhat reasonable compromise.

2. Pigouvian tax: A tax created to address environmental problems.

3. Windfall tax: A tax policy applying only to companies, imposing an additional tax on excess profits when they earn unexpectedly large gains because of external circumstances rather than special efforts or improvements in productivity.

Taxes are an important mechanism that allows society to function properly. As the saying goes, “Nothing is certain except death and taxes.” Taxes are inseparable from our lives and a central source of funding for running a country. But when you look at different taxes one by one, some seem unusual or difficult to understand intuitively from an ordinary person’s perspective. As taxpaying members of a country, it is natural for us to care about the criteria used to collect taxes and where the money goes. The government, too, has a responsibility to use taxpayers’ money reasonably and disclose both the process and the results transparently.

Still, even if a tax serves a good purpose, I do not think the government needs to intervene excessively in the market. That may sound contradictory, but no matter how I think about it, I have seen far more examples of taxes being used badly than well. So much is wasted on incomprehensible spending, corruption, misconduct, and abuses of authority. (The Yeosu Island Expo, the Saemangeum Jamboree, KRW 5 trillion in financial incentives for Gwangju…)

Conclusion: Whoever invented sin taxes is the real worst of the worst…
