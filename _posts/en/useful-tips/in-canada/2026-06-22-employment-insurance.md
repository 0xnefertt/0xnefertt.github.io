> This post provides general information as of July 22, 2026, and is not legal advice. Different rules may apply depending on occupation, collective agreements, federally regulated workplaces, averaging agreements, and other circumstances.

When working in BC, it is important first to determine whether you are an **employee or an independent contractor**. Simply having the word contractor in your contract does not settle your status; it may be assessed based on the actual working relationship. The BC Employment Standards Act generally applies to employees and does not apply to independent contractors.

Employment status is the most important point.

That is because whether protections such as minimum wage, overtime, and vacation pay apply can depend on whether you are an employee or a contractor.

Independent contractors generally handle their own taxes and CPP. EI does not apply automatically in the same way as it does for employees, and EI special benefits for self-employed people have separate registration requirements and conditions.

When joining as an employee: Offer Letter + Employment Agreement.

When working as a contractor: Independent Contractor Agreement / Service Agreement.

For employees, basic protections do not disappear simply because the role is called full-time, part-time, temporary, or permanent. However, there are exceptions for managers and certain occupations.

The second point is wage information.

- Minimum wage = the minimum hourly wage.

  It varies by province.

- Pay period = the period used to calculate wages. In BC, wages must generally be paid at least twice a month, and a pay period cannot exceed 16 days.

- Paystub = wages before tax.

- Tips = gratuities.

- Final pay = the timing of the final wage payment after resignation or dismissal.

- Record keeping = working hours.

Under BC's general rules, overtime pay applies to work exceeding <strong>eight hours a day</strong> or <strong>40 hours a week</strong>.

- Daily overtime (over eight hours a day) / Weekly overtime (over 40 hours a week).

- Double time (over 12 hours a day).

- Split shift (when a day's work is divided into separate shifts).

- Meal break (time for meals/rest).

The same applies to part-time and full-time employees.

Next, you should know about vacation pay and holiday pay.

In BC, after five days of employment, vacation pay generally accrues at a minimum of 4% of total wages. After five consecutive years of employment, the minimum is 6%.

**Pay Matters**

In Canada/BC, salary negotiations themselves are not a company's legal obligation. There is no law requiring a company to hold an annual salary negotiation or raise salaries by the rate of inflation. However, an adjustment may become mandatory when the minimum wage rises, an increase is promised in the employment contract, a role changes with a new pay agreement, or company policies require it. Put simply, salary increases are more a matter of negotiation and market rates than law. They are a negotiation, not a right.

Conversely, a significant unilateral change to wages or working hours may raise an issue of constructive dismissal, depending on the contract and the facts. Because this requires an individual assessment, it is safer to check with the Employment Standards Branch or an employment lawyer.

Overview

| Issue                                           | First Point of Contact            | Additional Contact     |
| ----------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------- | ---------------------- |
| Unpaid wages, vacation pay, or overtime issues  | BC Employment Standards Branch    |                        |
| EI applications, refusals, or ROE issues        | Service Canada                    |                        |
| Workplace injury                                | WorkSafeBC                        |                        |
| Discrimination or human rights violations       | BC Human Rights Tribunal / Clinic |                        |
| Problems in a unionized workplace               | Union rep                         | Labour Relations Board |
| Dismissal compensation, contracts, or severance | Employment lawyer                 |                        |
| Tax matters                                     | CRA                               |                        |

Whatever the issue, documents you can submit as evidence are important.
