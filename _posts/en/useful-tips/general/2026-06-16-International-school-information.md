The original purpose of international schools was quite practical. They were created so that children of diplomats, expatriate employees, staff of foreign companies, missionaries, and families who moved frequently between countries could continue their education wherever they went. Over time, they came to be seen as a route to a global elite education. In particular, parents usually send their children to international schools for the following reasons.

1. An English-speaking environment
2. Admission to universities abroad
3. Avoiding Korea's competitive entrance-exam system
4. Networking
5. An approach to education centered on discussion, presentations, and research
6. Giving their children a wider range of choices

There may be many reasons, but there is no doubt that the fundamental motivation starts with wanting one's own child to do even a little better.

International schools are categorized according to <strong>which country's curriculum</strong> they follow. Entry ages and graduation structures vary somewhat by curriculum, but they usually have a K–12 system extending from kindergarten through high school graduation. The subjects, assessment methods, teaching methods, and approach to university preparation differ from those of ordinary Korean schools.

Typical curricula include:

- American: American Curriculum, AP, SAT/ACT
- British: British Curriculum, IGCSE, A-Level
- IB: PYP, MYP, DP
- Canadian: Ontario, BC, Alberta Curriculum, and others
- Australian: Australian Curriculum
- European: French, German, Swiss, and others
- Hybrid: American + IB, British + IB
- Korean: Korea's national curriculum, school grades, and the CSAT

In other words, the curriculum you choose also changes the direction of university admissions. Among these, the IB is relatively versatile and widely used for admission to universities in the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada, Europe, and Asia.

There are generally four characteristics of an international school. First, it follows an overseas curriculum. Second, most classes are taught in English or another foreign language. Third, it has international or curriculum accreditation from organizations such as WASC, CIS, NEASC, IB, or Cambridge International. Fourth, it has a system for transcripts, recommendations, counseling, and essay guidance for admission to universities abroad.

In one sentence, an international school should be <strong>"a school that operates an overseas curriculum primarily in English, with international accreditation and a system for admission to universities abroad."</strong>

In Korea, the terms international school, foreign school, foreign educational institution, and alternative school are used interchangeably, making them easy to confuse. There are often said to be seven "authorized international schools that Korean nationals can enter without a history of living abroad": Chadwick International, Daegu International School, Calvin Manitoba International School, NLCS Jeju, Branksome Hall Asia, St. Johnsbury Academy Jeju, and KIS Jeju High School.

Foreign schools, on the other hand, are different in nature. Well-known foreign schools such as Seoul Foreign School, Seoul International School, and Yongsan International School are schools operating with education-office authorization, rather than academies outside the formal system. However, because their original purpose is to educate foreign children, Korean nationals often face additional admission requirements, such as a history of residence abroad.

Diplomas also need to be distinguished. Graduating from a foreign or international school may provide a school diploma, a High School Diploma, an IB Diploma, or A-Level results. However, this does not automatically mean recognition as a high school graduate in the Republic of Korea. Domestic academic recognition can depend on the school's authorization category, its designation for academic recognition, and completion of Korean language and social studies/Korean history courses.

Particular care is needed with unauthorized international schools or alternative English-language schools. Even if a school has "International School" in its name, its qualifications may not be recognized domestically. In that case, only middle school or an earlier level of education may be recognized in Korea, and a qualification examination may be needed for admission to a Korean university. When choosing a school, therefore, be sure to check domestic academic recognition, the type of diploma, overseas university admission results, and international accreditation.

While Switzerland, the United Kingdom, and the United States were popular destinations for international schools in the past, Malaysia, Singapore, India, and Dubai are attracting considerable attention these days.

## Why Do English-Speaking Countries Need International Schools?

In Korea, one of the biggest reasons for choosing an international school is an "English-speaking environment." But in countries such as Canada, the United States, the United Kingdom, and Australia, where English is already an official language, international schools mean something a little different. There, they are less about simply learning English and more about admitting international students, offering an international curriculum such as the IB, or providing boarding facilities.

Seen this way, international schools in Korea are, in some respects, used for purposes somewhat different from their original intent. They were originally created to maintain continuity in education for the children of diplomats, expatriate employees, foreign-company staff, and families with frequent international moves. In Korea, however, English education, overseas university admission, avoidance of the Korean entrance-exam system, social mobility, and networks have become strongly attached to that purpose.

Of course, being an English-speaking country does not mean there is no educational competition. Private tutoring, prestigious private schools, and a strong emphasis on education certainly exist in British Columbia, Canada, too. However, rather than "international schools" functioning as a major separate category as they do in Korea, the structure is closer to public-school international student programs, private/independent schools, IB schools, and boarding schools.

The following are often mentioned among BC's well-known private schools:

- St. George’s School
- Crofton House School
- York House School
- West Point Grey Academy
- Collingwood School
- Mulgrave School
- Stratford Hall
- Brookes Westshore
- Bodwell High School
- Pearson College UWC

In other words, international schools in English-speaking countries are less "schools for learning English" and more schools with international curricula, international students, boarding, and university admission systems. That makes me wonder whether immigrating or studying abroad at a young age is, in practice, much like attending an international school, since students use English and follow that country's curriculum.

## Tuition

![International school tuition information](/assets/img/posts/International-school-information.png)

Tuition at international schools and private schools abroad generally starts in the KRW 30 million range per year. Admission fees, uniforms, textbooks, activity fees, donations, and transportation may be added. Including boarding, you should budget around KRW 50 million to KRW 100 million per year. Add vacation programs and university consulting, and an enormous amount of money is needed. Sending a child to an international school means committing a tremendous amount of funding. It is not easy.
