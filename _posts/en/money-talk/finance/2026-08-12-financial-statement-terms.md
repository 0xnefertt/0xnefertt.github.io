When researching a company or reading economic and current-affairs news, unfamiliar terms often come up.

Many sound familiar but are easy to confuse, so I decided to organize them in a post.

(Writing this with help from ChatGPT…)

I believe I will get used to these terms, too, if I see them often enough.

---

\* Revenue = How much was sold = Total revenue

\* Cost of goods sold = The cost of the goods

\* Gross profit = Money left from the products themselves = Revenue - Cost of goods sold

\* Selling, general, and administrative expenses = Company operating expenses (salaries, advertising, rent, travel, etc.)

\* Operating profit = Profit earned from the core business

\* Operating loss = Negative operating profit

\* Profit before tax = Final profit before paying taxes

\* Net income = Final profit after accounting for taxes as well

\* Net loss = A loss on the bottom line

\* Operating profit margin = Operating profit / Revenue

\* Net profit margin = Net income / Revenue

For example: **Revenue of KRW 10 billion → Operating profit of KRW 1 billion → Net income of KRW 600 million**.

This means **the company sold KRW 10 billion worth of goods or services, earned KRW 1 billion from its core business, and ultimately had KRW 600 million left after interest, taxes, and other items**.

\* Profit/surplus = Money left over

\* Loss/deficit = Money lost

Operating profit and net income

\* Non-operating income = Money earned outside the core business

\* Non-operating expenses = Expenses incurred outside the core business

\* Interest income = Interest earned on deposits and similar assets

\* Interest expense = Interest on loans and similar obligations

\* Foreign-exchange gain = Money earned from exchange-rate movements

\* Foreign-exchange loss = Money lost from exchange-rate movements

\* Foreign-currency transaction gain/loss = Gains or losses arising from foreign-currency transactions

**\* Gain on disposal of tangible assets = Profit from selling buildings, machinery, and similar assets**

**\* Gain on disposal of investment assets = Profit from selling investment assets**

\* Impairment loss = An accounting loss recognizing a significant decline in an asset’s value

\* Corporate income tax expense = Income tax paid by a company

If operating profit is KRW 3 billion but net income is only KRW 500 million, check whether interest, foreign-exchange losses, taxes, or one-time losses were large. Conversely, if operating profit is KRW 3 billion but net income is KRW 7 billion, substantial gains may have occurred outside the core business, such as property sales or investment gains.

Is the company financially sound?

\* Assets = The total economic value a company owns (Liabilities + Equity)

\* Liabilities = What the company owes = Debt

\* Equity = Assets - Liabilities

\* Share capital = The basic capital initially invested by shareholders

\* Retained earnings = Money accumulated in the company = Accumulated profits

\* Debt-to-equity ratio = Liabilities / Equity

\* Equity ratio = Equity / Assets

\* Current assets = Assets that can be converted to cash within one year (cash, receivables, inventory, etc.)

\* Current liabilities = Liabilities payable within one year = Short-term debt

\* Current ratio = Current assets / Current liabilities

\* Borrowings = Money borrowed from financial institutions and others = Loans

\* Net debt = Borrowings - Cash and cash equivalents

For example, if a company has KRW 10 billion in assets and KRW 3 billion in debt, its own funds—equity—amount to KRW 7 billion.

**Terms used in stocks**

\* Market capitalization = Current share price × Number of shares outstanding

\* ROE = Net income relative to equity

\* ROA = How much the company earns relative to its total assets

\* PER = An indicator showing the multiple of earnings at which a stock trades

\* PBR = An indicator showing the multiple of net asset value at which a stock trades

\* EPS = Net income per share

\* BPS = Net assets per share

\* Dividend = A portion of the money a company earns that is distributed to shareholders

\* Dividend yield = Dividends as a proportion of the current share price

Income statement: A statement showing how much a company sold, how much it spent, and how much it ultimately earned or lost over a period.

Balance sheet: A statement showing the assets a company owns, the liabilities it owes, and the equity that is actually its own at a particular point in time.

Cash flow statement: A statement showing where cash came from and where it went over a period.

Income statement = Does it earn well?

Balance sheet = Is it financially solid?

Cash flow statement = Is actual cash flowing well?

I think that is a useful way to remember them.
