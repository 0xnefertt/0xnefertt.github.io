Let’s learn about game theory.

Game theory = a theory that studies situations in which several people make strategic decisions while anticipating one another’s actions.

For example, whether I lower my prices depends on how a competing company sets its prices.

Game theory usually has four basic components:

1\. Player: The decision-maker. This may be an individual, a company, a country, and so on.

2\. Strategy: An action available to a player.

3\. Payoff: The result obtained from choosing each strategy. This may be money, profit, utility, a win or loss, and so on.

4\. Information: How much is known about the other player’s actions or circumstances.

The most famous example is the **prisoner’s dilemma**.

Two criminals, A and B, each make a choice.

<table><tbody><tr><td></td><td><p>B stays silent</p></td><td><p>B betrays</p></td></tr><tr><td><p>A stays silent</p></td><td><p>A -1 / B - 1</p></td><td><p>A - 10 / B 0</p></td></tr><tr><td><p>A betrays</p></td><td><p>A 0 / B - 10</p></td><td><p>A -5 / B -5</p></td></tr></tbody></table>

If both stay silent, the result is -1, -1, close to the best outcome for them together.

But from A’s perspective, betrayal is better whether B stays silent or betrays. The same is true for B.

So they may ultimately arrive at A betrays + B betrays = (-5, -5).

**In other words, although a better outcome exists for both, pursuing their individual interests leads them to a worse outcome.**

An important concept here is the **dominant strategy**.

\= A strategy that is advantageous to me regardless of what the other person does.

In the prisoner’s dilemma above, betrayal is the dominant strategy.

And one of the most important concepts in game theory is **Nash equilibrium**.

\= A state in which changing my strategy alone cannot improve my outcome if everyone else’s strategy stays the same.

In the example above, “betray / betray” is the Nash equilibrium.

If A alone switches to silence, the result changes from -5 to -10, making A worse off. The same applies to B, so neither has a reason to change strategy alone.

A Nash equilibrium does not mean the best outcome for society.

Game theory appears incredibly often in real life, including business competition, international relations, and everyday life.

Let’s explore game theory through a second example: a **zero-sum game**.

As the name suggests, a zero-sum game is one in which one side loses exactly what the other gains, so the total payoff is zero.

The key is that the pie does not grow or shrink. The question is who gets more of an already fixed pie.

The other player’s gain is my loss.

Most situations involving winners and losers or competition are close to zero-sum games. Rock-paper-scissors is another example.

Unlike the prisoner’s dilemma, the more important question in a zero-sum game is, “**How much can I take without letting the other person take it from me?**”

This introduces the concept of **minimax**.

\= A strategy that minimizes my losses in the worst-case scenario.

Where the prisoner’s dilemma and rock-paper-scissors (a zero-sum game) are generally games in which choices are made simultaneously, **a sequential game is one in which someone acts first and the next person responds after observing that action**.

The key point is that “who moves first changes strategies and decisions.”

Chess is a major example, and this also happens frequently in everyday life.

Two new cafés, A and B, are considering opening.

A chooses first.

A: Should I open a café or not?

If A opens, B must decide next.

B: Should I open with lower prices, or simply compete?

One person acts first, and the other acts after seeing that move. That is the core of a sequential game.
