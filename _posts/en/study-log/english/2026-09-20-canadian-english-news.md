I happened to see an Instagram video discussing serious literacy problems among younger generations, and it struck a nerve. I waste many hours on social media every day. Perhaps that is why I sometimes feel my own reading comprehension declining. I have developed a habit of speaking with all-purpose pronouns such as “this,” “that,” and “that thing.” My speech is losing its logical structure, and longer pieces are becoming harder to read.

So I plan to read current-affairs articles and commentary. I will read English-language newspapers to study English at the same time. It may be easy to think, “Why not just read anything written in English?” But I think choosing the newspaper also matters.

In Korea, there are conservative newspapers commonly grouped as **Cho-Joong-Dong (Chosun Ilbo, JoongAng Ilbo, and Dong-A Ilbo)**. On the progressive side, there is **Han-Kyung-Oh (Hankyoreh, Kyunghyang Shinmun, and OhmyNews)**.

You may want to think that the news should report only facts, but reality is different. Media organizations and newspapers develop identities shaped by the political circumstances of their founding. They inevitably carry historical, economic, social, and political leanings.

In other words, it is important to subscribe to outlets whose values fit your own. Writing has an invisible but formidable power. Reading, learning, and absorbing information may give me biased views without my even realizing it. It is similar to indoctrination.

Let’s look at some well-known newspapers. **The New York Times, The Washington Post, and The Guardian** are generally considered closer to the progressive or liberal side, while **Fox News** is considered closer to the Republican or conservative side. **Reuters and Bloomberg** are regarded as relatively centrist. Britain’s **The Times** has traditionally been viewed as center-right or conservative, although its political positions have varied by period and election.
